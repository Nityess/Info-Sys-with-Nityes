Section 1IT in the Business Environment›Topic 1.4
1.4

What makes information valuable

The same information can be worth a lot or nothing. Its value depends on four factors, SEAR teaching aid. And information that is never used has zero value. Turn the dials.

SEAR

Change one factor at a time

The report is identical in every setting. Only its value changes.

Key points

S · Source
External information from a respected, well-known source is more valuable because you can trust it. Internal information from a source known to be accurate and efficient is more reliable. An unknown source cannot be relied on with confidence.
E · Ease of assimilation
Colour, graphics, infographics and dashboards make information easier to understand, so decisions are faster.
A · Accessibility
Information that is easy to access saves time and effort. Hard-to-find information is less valuable.
R · Relevance
Value is in the eyes of the beholder. Information on a new type of plastic is very valuable to a car manufacturer and worthless to a software house.
Not scarcity
Unlike most commodities, the value of information is not based on scarcity. Most managers complain of too much information, not too little.

IT delivery and IT support

The IT function has two teams. IT delivery makes sure IT services RUN. IT support makes sure the infrastructure WORKS. The aim of both: add value through appropriate technology. Dispatch each event to the right team.

0 / 0
Key difference (Q29)

Delivery = service to users. Support = system maintenance.

IT delivery is what users get. IT support is how the systems are kept running, stable and secure.

How management makes IT add value: MPSS teaching aid

M · Monitoring
Stay aware of rapid IT developments (cloud, AI, automation). Build a network of contacts and information sources. Missing new technology means missing business opportunities.
P · Planning
IT changes need careful planning and preparation. Disruption from unplanned changes can cripple operations.
S · Structure
Prioritise and document IT tasks. Maintain a rolling programme of "business as usual" and "forward change" activities.
S · Staffing and skills
Recruit and retain the right people, for example data science skills, matched to appropriate roles.

Outsourcing and external support

Outsourcing: contracting out a specific business function to an external provider instead of doing it in-house. Move the IT out, then see what changes.

In-house

The company runs its own IT

Switch to outsourced IT to see the benefits and the challenges.

BenefitsFocus on core activities. Better IT services than in-house staff could deliver. Often more cost-effective and efficient.
ChallengesMore formality (contracts, SLAs). Arm's length management, less direct control. Contractual issues if the provider underperforms.

External support

User groups and peer support
Many organisations use the SAME hardware, software and services, so businesses can tap into user groups and peer support (networking) communities. Sharing experiences and solutions saves time, money and headaches.

2.2

When is financial information useful?

Financial information is useful when it helps users make decisions and shows management's stewardship of the resources entrusted to them. Follow it from transaction to use.

Tap any statement or purpose to read what it shows.
Underlying assumptions

Accrual basis + going concern

For financial information to be useful, it is prepared on the accrual basis and the going concern assumption, presented through the three statements, and used for decisions and stewardship.

Illustrative example.
Accrual basis

Key terms

Accrual basis
Transactions are recorded when they occur, not when cash is received or paid. They are reported in the period to which they relate.
Going concern
The business is assumed to continue operating for the foreseeable future.
Economic phenomena
Primary users need information about economic resources (what the business owns), claims against the entity (what it owes) and changes in resources and claims (effects of transactions and events).
How it is communicated
Information systems produce operational, financial and compliance reports to run and control the business. Flows go downwards (management to employees), upwards (employees to management), across (department to department) and externally (customers, suppliers, regulators, shareholders).

Spreadsheets

A spreadsheet is an application that lets users store and analyse data in a grid of cells arranged in columns and rows. Examples: Microsoft Excel, Google Sheets, Apple Numbers. Click any cell; change the numbers.

A1
Branch figures are illustrative. Edit any number in the formula bar.
Cell A1

Key points

Cell reference
Each cell has a unique reference, e.g. A4 = column A, row 4.
Text
Words or numbers NOT used for calculations, e.g. headings and labels.
Values
Numbers used IN calculations, e.g. sales figures and quantities.
Formulae
Expressions that calculate values using other cells, e.g. =B4+B5.
Functions
Predefined formulae for common operations, e.g. =SUM(B4:B10), =AVERAGE(...). Modern spreadsheets offer dozens: arithmetical, financial and statistical.
Workbook
Several worksheets in one workbook (e.g. one per branch). Formulae in one worksheet can reference data in another.
Flexible, so plan it
Users design the structure themselves, so spreadsheets must be planned and constructed carefully to avoid output errors. Small businesses often keep their entire accounting records in spreadsheets.
