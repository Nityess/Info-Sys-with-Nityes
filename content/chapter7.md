## Topic 1.2 - Types of Information

**Master Teaching Content | ICAN · Exam-focused**

### 🎯 Learning objectives

By the end of this topic, students should be able to:

- Explain how information is classified according to its use.
- Identify the four types of information in the study material.
- Understand who uses each type and its main characteristics.
- Identify the correct type from a practical business scenario.
- Avoid common MCQ traps involving strategic, tactical and operational information.

### 1. Start with a simple business example

Imagine a bank with branches across Nepal. Different employees need different types of information:

- The Board wants to know whether the bank should expand into new markets over the next five years.
- A regional manager wants monthly performance reports to improve branch performance.
- A branch manager needs daily reports showing unauthorised overdrafts.
- The planning team needs information to prepare next year's budget.

Notice something important: the same organisation needs different information for different purposes and different levels of management.

That is why information is classified according to its use.

The study material identifies four types:

1. Strategic information
2. Tactical information
3. Operational information
4. Planning information

### 2. Understand the four types

#### 1. Strategic Information

**Who uses it?** Board of Directors / Senior Managers. **Long-term focus.**

**Meaning:** Strategic information helps senior management make major decisions about the long-term direction of the organisation.

Key characteristics:

- Long-term in nature.
- Often based on estimates.
- Provides a broad picture of the organisation.
- Supports major business decisions.

*Example from a business situation:* The Board of a manufacturing company wants to decide whether to expand production capacity over the next five years. It needs information about future demand, investment requirements and available resources. That is strategic information.

**Memory clue:** Strategic = Big picture + Long-term + Major decisions.

**Important exam point:** Strategic information is often based on estimates. It is not necessarily highly accurate or routine.

#### 2. Tactical Information

**Who uses it?** Middle Managers. **Short-term focus.**

**Meaning:** Tactical information helps middle managers deal with short-term issues by using analysed data.

Key characteristics:

- Short-term in nature.
- Based on analysed data.
- Deals with short-term issues.

*Example:* A factory manager reviews monthly variance reports to understand why production costs have increased and what needs to be improved. That is tactical information.

**Memory clue:** Tactical = Middle management + Analysed data + Short-term issues.

#### 3. Operational Information

**Who uses it?** Front-line Staff. **Day-to-day focus.**

**Meaning:** Operational information supports the routine, day-to-day activities of an organisation.

Key characteristics:

- Day-to-day in nature.
- Routine.
- Very detailed.
- Mostly accurate.

*Examples:*

- Information about how many operatives are needed on a particular shift.
- Daily branch reports used to monitor unauthorised overdrafts.

These are operational because they relate to detailed, routine and day-to-day activities.

**Memory clue:** Operational = Front-line staff + Daily activities + Detailed information.

**Exam alert:** Daily branch reports monitoring unauthorised overdrafts are operational information.

#### 4. Planning Information

**Who uses it?** Planning Teams. **Implementation focus.**

**Meaning:** Planning information focuses on the steps required to implement a plan.

The study material highlights:

- Implementation steps.
- Available resources.
- Time-scales.

*Example:* A planning team prepares a budget for the next financial year, considering the resources required and the time needed to implement activities. That is planning information.

**Memory clue:** Planning = Implementation steps + Resources + Time-scales.

### 3. Compare all four - the exam revision table

| Type | Who uses it? | Main characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic | Board / Senior Managers | Long-term, often estimates, broad picture, major decisions |
| Tactical | Middle Managers | Short-term, analysed data, short-term issues |
| Operational | Front-line Staff | Day-to-day, routine, very detailed, mostly accurate |
| Planning | Planning Teams | Implementation steps, resources, time-scales |

### 4. The management hierarchy - visualise the difference

A useful way to remember the first three types is to imagine three levels of management.

- **STRATEGIC** - Board / Senior Managers. *Where should the organisation go in the long term?* Long-term · Broad picture.
- **TACTICAL** - Middle Managers. *What short-term actions are needed?* Analysed data · Short-term.
- **OPERATIONAL** - Front-line Staff. *What needs to happen in today's operations?* Daily · Routine · Detailed.

*Conceptual management hierarchy for remembering the three information types.*

**Where does Planning Information fit?**

Planning information is separately identified in the study material. It focuses on implementation steps, resources and time-scales, and is used by planning teams. Do not force it into the three-level hierarchy above.

### 5. One business, four types of information

Let's use one example to make the difference crystal clear. A bank is considering expanding its operations.

| Situation | Correct type | Why? |
|---|---|---|
| Board considers opening branches across Nepal over the next five years. | Strategic | Long-term major decision |
| Regional manager reviews monthly branch variance reports. | Tactical | Analysed data for short-term issues |
| Branch staff review daily unauthorised overdraft reports. | Operational | Routine day-to-day information |
| Planning team prepares next year's budget and resource requirements. | Planning | Implementation, resources and time-scales |

**Teaching tip:** Ask students to identify the purpose and user of the information, not merely look for words such as "report", "budget" or "daily".

### 6. Common exam traps ⚠️

**Trap 1: Strategic information is not necessarily highly accurate.**

The study material says strategic information is often based on estimates and provides a broad picture.

- Correct: Often based on estimates
- Incorrect: Always very detailed and highly accurate

**Trap 2: Daily branch reports are operational.**

Daily reports used to monitor unauthorised overdrafts are operational information, not strategic or tactical.

- Correct: Operational

**Trap 3: Tactical information is used by middle managers.**

Monthly factory variance reports are a good example of tactical information.

- Correct: Tactical

**Trap 4: Planning information is a separate category in this chapter.**

Do not confuse the general activity of planning with the specific classification called Planning Information.

**Remember:** Planning teams · Implementation · Resources · Time-scales

## Topic 1.3 - Qualities of Good Information (ACCURATE)

**New topic | Exam-focused**

This is a key concept-based topic. The goal is not simply to memorise eight words. Students should understand what makes information useful to a manager, recognise poor-quality information in business situations, and explain the relevant characteristic in an exam answer.

We will use the exact eight characteristics and terminology from the study material. All practice scenarios and questions below will be newly created, not copied from the PDF.

### 1. Start with a simple business situation

Imagine a company has five branches across Nepal. The CEO receives a monthly performance report to decide which branches need attention.

But the report has problems:

- Some figures are calculated incorrectly.
- The report excludes two branches.
- It contains 5,000 lines of transaction details.
- It arrives three weeks after the month ends.
- It includes information that has nothing to do with the CEO's decision.

Would this report help the CEO make a good decision? Obviously not. The company has information, but it does not have good-quality information.

**Core idea:** Information is valuable only when it is reliable, sufficient, useful to the intended person, and available when needed.

### 2. The ACCURATE mnemonic

**A C C U R A T E - Eight qualities of good information**

| Letter | Quality | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| A | Accurate | Correct figures and classification |
| C | Complete | All necessary information included |
| C | Cost-beneficial | Benefits justify the cost |
| U | User-targeted | Designed for the intended user |
| R | Relevant | Only decision-useful information |
| A | Authoritative | Reliable and credible source |
| T | Timely | Available when needed |
| E | Easy to use | Clear, suitable presentation |

Let's understand each characteristic through one continuing business example.

### 3. Understand all eight characteristics

#### A - Accurate

Information should be correct and free from errors.

The study material highlights:

- Figures should add up.
- Rounding must be appropriate.
- There should be no typographical errors.
- Items must be allocated to the correct category.
- Assumptions should be stated for uncertain information.

*Business example:* A branch performance report shows revenue of NPR 25 lakh, but the underlying ledger supports NPR 22 lakh. The report is inaccurate.

**Remember:** Correct figures + correct classification + appropriate assumptions.

**Important nuance:** Management information should be sufficiently accurate for its purpose. Chasing absolute 100% accuracy can create unnecessary delays.

#### C - Complete

Information should include everything necessary for the purpose it is intended to serve.

The study material specifically highlights:

- All necessary information should be included.
- Relevant external data should be included.
- Comparative or prior-year figures should be present.

*Business example:* The CEO receives a report showing this year's sales but no previous-year figures. If the CEO needs to assess growth, the report is incomplete.

**Remember:** Nothing important is missing.

#### C - Cost-beneficial

The benefit of obtaining and presenting information should justify its cost.

The study material also highlights efficient collection methods and avoiding unnecessary use of users' time.

*Business example:* A company spends NPR 5 lakh preparing a highly detailed report to support a decision that can only generate an expected benefit of NPR 50,000. Producing that report may not be cost-beneficial.

**Remember:** Benefit should justify cost.

#### U - User-targeted

Information must be tailored to the needs of the specific person who will use it.

The study material makes an important distinction:

- Senior managers → summaries.
- Junior managers → detailed breakdowns.

*Business example:*

- CEO needs a dashboard showing revenue, profitability and key performance indicators.
- Branch accountant needs detailed transaction-level information.

Sending the same detailed transaction listing to both users is not user-targeted.

**Remember:** Right information for the right person.

#### R - Relevant

Information should relate directly to the decision being made. Unnecessary information should be omitted, even if it seems interesting. Excess information can cause confusion.

*Business example:* A regional manager wants to compare branch profitability. A report containing every employee's birthday and personal contact details is irrelevant to that decision.

**Remember:** Include what is needed for the decision; exclude what is not.

#### A - Authoritative

Information should come from a reliable and credible source.

The study material specifically warns that internet sources should be checked for authority.

*Business example:* A company uses an official government publication for tax-related market research. Another company uses an anonymous website with no identifiable source. The latter raises concerns about authority.

**Remember:** Can we trust the source?

#### T - Timely

Information should be available when it is needed.

- Annual reports are too infrequent for day-to-day control.
- Delayed information loses its value.
- Information must arrive early enough to support the intended decision.

*Business example:* A bank's management needs to monitor unauthorised overdrafts daily. Receiving the report three weeks later may be too late to take corrective action.

**Remember:** Right information, at the right time.

#### E - Easy to use

Information should be clearly presented, not excessively long, and delivered through the right medium or communication channel.

Possible channels include email, telephone, hard-copy reports and dashboards.

*Business example:* A colour-coded dashboard that highlights problem branches is easier to use than a 50-page printout of raw data.

**Remember:** Easy to understand, interpret and use.

### 4. The most important distinctions students must understand

This is where students can get confused in scenario-based questions. Let's separate the similar characteristics.

#### User-targeted vs Relevant

| User-targeted | Relevant |
|---|---|
| Focuses on who receives information. | Focuses on what information is included. |
| Is it designed for the intended user? | Is it useful for the decision? |
| CEO receives a summary; accountant receives details. | A profitability report excludes unrelated personal data. |

**Simple distinction:** User-targeted = right person. Relevant = right content.

#### Complete vs Relevant

| Complete | Relevant |
|---|---|
| Includes all necessary information. | Excludes unnecessary information. |
| Nothing important is missing. | Nothing unnecessary is added. |

A report can be complete but still contain too much irrelevant information.

#### Accurate vs Authoritative

| Accurate | Authoritative |
|---|---|
| Focuses on correctness of information. | Focuses on reliability of the source. |
| Are the figures correct? | Can the source be trusted? |

A report can come from a credible source but still contain calculation errors.

#### Timely vs Easy to use

| Timely | Easy to use |
|---|---|
| Available when required. | Presented in a usable manner. |
| Is it received on time? | Can the user understand it easily? |

A beautifully designed report delivered after the decision has been made is still not timely.

### 5. A practical way to remember all eight

Imagine you are preparing a monthly MIS report for the CEO.

**The CEO's report checklist**

- **Accurate:** Are the figures correct?
- **Complete:** Have I included all necessary figures and comparisons?
- **Cost-beneficial:** Is preparing this report worth its cost?
- **User-targeted:** Is this report designed for the CEO?
- **Relevant:** Does every item help with the decision?
- **Authoritative:** Are my sources reliable?
- **Timely:** Will the CEO receive it before making the decision?
- **Easy to use:** Can the CEO understand it quickly?

### 6. Four qualities frequently overlooked in practice

The study material identifies four qualities that are commonly ignored:

- **User-targeted:** Systems are not designed around the specific needs of users.
- **Relevant:** Unnecessary information is included.
- **Easy to use:** Reports are poorly structured, excessively long or presented in unsuitable formats.
- **Timely:** Information is provided too late to support the decision.

This is useful for both understanding and scenario-based exam preparation.

### 8. How to write an exam-oriented answer

**Possible question:** Explain the characteristics of good-quality information.

**Suggested answer:**

Good-quality information should possess eight key characteristics, represented by the mnemonic ACCURATE:

- **Accurate:** Information should contain correct figures, appropriate rounding and correct classification, without errors.
- **Complete:** It should include all necessary information, including relevant external and comparative data.
- **Cost-beneficial:** The benefits obtained from information should justify the cost of collecting and presenting it.

- **Timely:** Information should be available when required to support decision-making.
- **Easy to use:** Information should be clearly presented, appropriately structured and delivered through a suitable medium.

## Topic 1.4 - What Makes Information Valuable? (SEAR)

**Next learning topic | Concept + application**

This topic is short, but conceptually important. Students need to understand that having information does not automatically mean that the information is valuable.

We will focus on the four factors identified in the study material:

1. Source
2. Ease of Assimilation
3. Accessibility
4. Relevance

All examples and practice questions below are newly created for teaching. No questions are copied from the source document.

### 1. Start with a real-life situation

Imagine two business owners are planning to open a new restaurant in Kathmandu. Both receive market research reports.

- **Report A:** Prepared by a recognised research organisation, presented through clear charts, available online and focused on restaurant customers.
- **Report B:** Prepared by an unknown website, presented in a confusing 100-page document, difficult to access and mainly discussing the software industry.

Both reports contain information. But are they equally valuable to the restaurant owners? Clearly not.

The value of information depends on whether it comes from a trustworthy source, can be understood, can be accessed easily and is useful to the particular user.

### 2. The four factors

**S E A R** - *A teaching-created memory aid for the four source factors*

| Letter | Factor | Question |
|---|---|---|
| S | Source | Can we trust where it comes from? |
| E | Ease of Assimilation | Can we understand it easily? |
| A | Accessibility | Can we obtain it easily? |
| R | Relevance | Is it useful to this user? |

**SEAR is only a classroom memory aid. The source document does not introduce this mnemonic.**

### 3. Understand each factor

#### 1 - Source

The value of information depends on where it comes from.

A respected and well-known source generally gives users greater confidence in the information.

*Example:* A company uses market data published by a recognised research organisation rather than relying on an unknown website.

**Think:** Who prepared this information? Can we trust the source?

**Key idea:** Trustworthiness of the source.

#### 2 - Ease of Assimilation

Information becomes more valuable when it is presented in a way that makes it easier to understand.

The study material identifies presentation methods such as:

- Colours
- Graphics
- Infographics
- Dashboards
- Sound
- Movement

*Example:* A CEO receives a visual dashboard showing revenue, expenses and profit trends instead of a long list of raw accounting data. The dashboard makes the information richer and easier to understand.

**Think:** How easily can the user understand what the information is telling them?

**Key idea:** Ease of understanding.

#### 3 - Accessibility

Information is more valuable when users can access it easily. If information is available in an accessible place, users save time and effort retrieving it.

*Example:* A company's management report is available through a secure online portal. Managers can access it whenever required instead of repeatedly requesting printed copies.

**Think:** How easily can the user obtain the information?

**Key idea:** Ease of access.

#### 4 - Relevance

The value of information depends on whether it is useful to the specific user.

The study material makes an important point: **the value of information is not based on scarcity.**

Information does not become valuable merely because it is rare or difficult to obtain. Its value depends on its usefulness to the particular user.

*Example:* Information about a newly developed lightweight plastic may be valuable to a car manufacturer. The same information may be irrelevant to a software development company. The information itself has not changed. The user has changed.

**Think:** Does this information actually help this particular user?

**Key idea:** Usefulness to the specific user.

### 4. Do not confuse these four factors

This comparison is especially useful for scenario-based questions.

| Factor | Main question | Clue in a scenario |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Where does it come from? | Recognised / unknown organisation |
| Ease of Assimilation | How is it understood? | Dashboard, charts, infographics |
| Accessibility | How easily can it be obtained? | Online availability, easy retrieval |
| Relevance | Is it useful to the user? | Useful for one business, irrelevant to another |

**The three most important distinctions**

**Ease of Assimilation vs Accessibility**

- Ease of Assimilation = Can I understand it?
- Accessibility = Can I obtain it?

A report may be very easy to understand but difficult to access.

**Source vs Relevance**

- Source = Can I trust it?
- Relevance = Is it useful to me?

A report from a respected organisation may still be irrelevant to a particular business decision.

**Relevance vs Scarcity**

Rare information is not automatically valuable. Its usefulness to the specific user determines its value.

### 5. Apply your understanding - business scenarios

Consider these situations:

- **Scenario A:** A company obtains economic data from a well-known international research agency. *Factor: Source*
- **Scenario B:** A finance manager receives a colour-coded dashboard instead of hundreds of pages of raw figures. *Factor: Ease of Assimilation*
- **Scenario C:** Branch managers can retrieve reports through an online management system without requesting them from head office. *Factor: Accessibility*
- **Scenario D:** Information about agricultural machinery is useful to a farming company but not useful to a hotel business. *Factor: Relevance*

### 7. Exam-oriented answer writing

**Possible question:** Explain the factors that determine the value of information.

**Suggested answer:**

The value of information depends on the following four factors:

- **Source:** Information obtained from a respected and well-known source can be relied upon with greater confidence.
- **Ease of Assimilation:** Information presented through colours, graphics, infographics and dashboards becomes richer and easier to understand.
- **Accessibility:** Information that is easily accessible saves users' time and effort in retrieving it.
- **Relevance:** Information is valuable when it is useful to a specific user. Its value does not depend on scarcity but on its usefulness to that user.

## Topic 1.5 - Assessing the Cost and Value of Information

**Next learning topic | Concept + numerical application**

This topic connects information with business decision-making and introduces a simple but important question:

**Is the information worth the money spent to obtain it?**

A business should not collect information simply because it can. It should consider whether that information will actually create value.

By the end of this topic, students should be able to:

- Explain why unused information has no actual value.
- Understand how information creates value through actions.
- Compare the extra benefits and extra costs of obtaining information.
- Calculate whether obtaining information is worthwhile.
- Explain why the cost of an individual MIS enquiry can be effectively zero.

### 1. Start with a business situation

Imagine a manufacturing company is considering whether to collect additional market information before launching a new product.

The company estimates:

- Additional information could help improve profit by NPR 90,000.
- Collecting that information would cost NPR 1,00,000.

Should the company obtain the information? At first glance, more information sounds useful. But look at the numbers:

| The information investment | Amount |
|---|---|
| Expected extra benefit | NPR 90,000 |
| Extra cost | NPR 1,00,000 |
| **Net value** | **− NPR 10,000** |

The expected benefit is lower than the cost. Obtaining this additional information is not worthwhile on these estimates.

**Simple lesson:** More information does not always mean more value.

### 2. Principle 1 - Information obtained but not used

The study material makes a fundamental point:

> Information that is obtained but not used has no actual value. Value only arises from actions taken based on that information.

Let's understand this.

Suppose a company spends NPR 50,000 collecting a market research report. The report identifies that demand for a particular product is increasing. However, management does nothing with the information.

- The report was obtained.
- Money was spent.
- Information was available.
- But no action was taken based on it.

Therefore, the information has created no actual value through action.

Now imagine management uses that report to increase production of the product and earns additional profit. The information has now helped the business take action and create value.

**How information creates value:**

Information obtained → Management uses information → Action taken → Value created

**Remember:** Information is not valuable merely because it exists. Its value arises when it supports actions.

### 3. Principle 2 - Extra benefits versus extra costs

Whether it is worthwhile to obtain additional information depends on two things:

1. Extra benefits expected from obtaining the information.
2. Extra costs incurred to obtain the information.

The decision is based on comparing these two amounts.

**Decision rule - The basic formula**

```
Net Value = Extra Benefits − Extra Costs
```

- **Positive:** Extra benefits exceed extra costs.
- **Negative:** Extra costs exceed extra benefits.
- **Zero:** Extra benefits equal extra costs.

For a simple decision-making exercise:

- Positive net value → obtaining information is worthwhile.
- Negative net value → obtaining information is not worthwhile.
- Zero net value → financially, the extra benefits and costs are equal.

#### Example 1 - Information is not worthwhile

The study material gives an example:

- Expected profit improvement = NPR 9,000
- Cost of collecting information = NPR 10,000

Calculation: `9,000 − 10,000 = −1,000`

**Negative net value.** The information is not worth obtaining because the cost exceeds the expected profit improvement by NPR 1,000.

#### Example 2 - Information is worthwhile

A retail company is considering additional market research.

| Particulars | Amount |
|---|---|
| Expected extra benefit | NPR 2,50,000 |
| Extra cost | NPR 1,50,000 |
| **Net value** | **NPR 1,00,000** |

`2,50,000 − 1,50,000 = 1,00,000`

**Positive net value.** The expected benefit exceeds the additional cost by NPR 1,00,000.

*Teaching example created for this lesson.*

### 4. Principle 3 - Cost of an individual MIS enquiry

This is a particularly interesting concept. Suppose a company has already purchased:

- A Management Information System (MIS).
- Enquiry software.
- The necessary infrastructure.

Now a manager logs into the existing system and requests a report about monthly sales.

What is the cost of that individual enquiry? According to the study material, the cost is **effectively zero**.

Why? Because the major costs have already been incurred.

**Understand the MIS cost concept**

**Step 1 - Initial investment:** The company purchases the MIS and enquiry software. These are largely fixed costs already incurred.

**Step 2 - Additional enquiry:** A manager requests another report using the existing system.

**Effectively zero marginal cost:** No meaningful additional cost is incurred for that individual enquiry under the situation described.

**Important distinction**

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Total MIS cost | The cost of purchasing and maintaining the system |
| Fixed costs already incurred | Costs that have already been paid |
| Marginal cost of an individual enquiry | Additional cost of making one more enquiry |

**Do not make this mistake:** The cost of the entire MIS is not zero. The cost of an individual additional enquiry using the existing system is effectively zero.

This distinction is important for understanding the concept.

## Topic 1.6 - IT Delivery and IT Support

**Next learning topic | Concept + scenario application**

This topic is about understanding two important responsibilities of an organisation's IT function:

- **IT Delivery:** Making sure IT services are delivered and available to the business.
- **IT Support:** Making sure the technology and infrastructure behind those services remain secure, reliable and functional.

The two activities may be performed by different teams, but both contribute to the overall objective of adding value to the organisation through appropriate technology.

### 1. Start with a practical business example

Imagine a bank operating 50 branches across Nepal. Its employees use:

- Banking software to process customer transactions.
- Computer networks to connect branches.
- Servers to store and process data.
- Reports to monitor daily transactions.
- Security controls to protect customer information.

Now imagine two situations.

**Situation A:** The bank's management needs daily transaction reports, but nobody extracts, prepares or distributes those reports.

**Situation B:** The banking software is available, but the network is not properly maintained, access controls are weak and technical incidents are not resolved.

Both situations create problems, but the nature of the responsibilities is different.

- Situation A concerns **IT Delivery**.
- Situation B concerns **IT Support**.

Let's understand both properly.

### 2. IT Delivery Activities

IT Delivery focuses on the provision and operation of IT services required by the organisation.

The study material identifies the following activities.

**IT Delivery - Think:** Delivering IT services to business users.

#### 1. IT Service Operations

Includes:

- Data extraction.
- Report production.
- Report distribution.

*Example:* Extracting transaction data from the banking system, preparing a daily report and distributing it to branch managers.

#### 2. Capacity Monitoring and Management

Ensuring that IT capacity is monitored and managed to meet business requirements.

*Example:* Monitoring whether the bank's systems can handle a significant increase in customer transactions.

#### 3. Customer Billing and Budgeting

Handling customer billing and budgeting activities related to IT services.

*Example:* Preparing budgets for IT services and managing billing for services provided to customers.

#### 4. Business Operations Availability to Agreed Service Levels

Ensuring that business operations remain available in accordance with agreed service levels.

*Example:* Ensuring that a banking service is available according to the availability level agreed with the business.

#### 5. Service Continuity / Contingency Management

Managing continuity arrangements and contingency plans so that IT services can continue or recover when disruptions occur.

*Example:* Having arrangements to continue essential banking services if the primary system becomes unavailable.

**Memory idea:** Delivery = Operations + Capacity + Billing/Budgeting + Availability + Continuity.

### 3. IT Support Activities

IT Support focuses on maintaining and protecting the organisation's IT infrastructure.

The study material identifies these activities.

**IT Support - Think:** Maintaining the technology behind IT services.

#### 1. Maintaining IT Infrastructure Configuration

Maintaining the configuration of:

- Hardware.
- Software.
- Networks.

*Example:* Maintaining the configuration of bank servers, computers, banking software and network equipment.

#### 2. Physical and Logical Integrity of Infrastructure

Protecting the physical and logical integrity of IT infrastructure.

*Example:* Protecting server equipment from physical damage and preventing unauthorised changes to system configurations.

#### 3. Security and Access Control

Ensuring appropriate security measures and access controls are maintained.

*Example:* Ensuring that only authorised bank employees can access sensitive banking systems.

#### 4. Incident Management

Preventing, investigating and resolving incidents.

*Example:* Investigating why a branch network has stopped working and resolving the technical problem.

**Memory idea:** Support = Configuration + Integrity + Security + Incident Management.

### 4. The most important comparison - IT Delivery vs IT Support

This is the central distinction students must understand.

| IT Delivery | IT Support |
|---|---|
| Delivers IT services. | Maintains IT infrastructure. |
| Focuses on service operations. | Focuses on infrastructure configuration. |
| Extracts and produces reports. | Maintains hardware, software and networks. |
| Monitors capacity. | Maintains physical and logical integrity. |
| Manages service availability and continuity. | Manages security, access and incidents. |

**A simple way to remember**

- **IT Delivery - SERVE:** Make IT services available to users.
- **IT Support - PROTECT:** Maintain and protect the IT infrastructure.

*Example:* Producing a banking report = Delivery. Maintaining the server that stores the banking data = Support.

**Test the distinction**

A bank's IT department performs the following activities:

- Extracts daily transaction reports → IT Delivery.
- Maintains the network connecting branches → IT Support.
- Distributes reports to management → IT Delivery.
- Controls access to the banking application → IT Support.
- Investigates a network incident → IT Support.
- Monitors IT service capacity → IT Delivery.

**Exam tip:** Don't classify an activity merely because it involves computers. Identify its actual purpose.

### 5. Management actions to keep the IT function effective

Having an IT department is not enough. Management must take appropriate actions to ensure that the IT function remains effective.

The study material identifies four actions.

**M P S S** - *Classroom memory aid, four management actions*

#### 1. Monitoring

Management should remain aware of rapid developments in IT, such as:

- Cloud computing.
- Automation.
- Intelligent systems.

**Why?** So the organisation does not miss opportunities arising from technological developments.

*Example:* Management monitors developments in AI automation to identify opportunities to improve accounting processes.

#### 2. Planning

Careful planning ensures that IT changes are implemented smoothly and without disrupting business operations.

*Example:* Before upgrading banking software, management plans the implementation to minimise disruption to banking services.

#### 3. Structure

IT tasks should be prioritised and documented.

Management should maintain a rolling programme covering:

- Business-as-usual activities.
- Forward-change activities.

*Example:* The IT department maintains a schedule for routine system maintenance alongside planned upgrades.

#### 4. Staffing & Skills

The organisation should recruit and retain employees with appropriate skills, including suitable data science skills, and match them to appropriate roles.

*Example:* Recruiting data analysts and IT specialists with the skills needed for the organisation's technology requirements.

**Understand the difference between the four**

| Action | Main focus |
|---|---|
| Monitoring | Stay aware of technological developments. |
| Planning | Implement changes smoothly. |
| Structure | Prioritise and document IT activities. |
| Staffing & Skills | Have the right people with the right skills. |

## Topic 1.7 - Outsourcing

**Section 1 · Next topic | Concept + exam application**

Let's start with a simple business situation.

Imagine a bank needs an IT system to manage its customer records, transactions and reports. The bank has two choices:

1. Recruit its own IT employees and manage the entire IT function internally.
2. Contract an external technology company to perform a specific IT function.

If the bank chooses the second option, it is using **outsourcing**.

### 1. What is Outsourcing?

**Source definition**

> Outsourcing is an arrangement where an organisation contracts out a specific business function to an external provider.

**Organisation → External Provider.** A specific business function is contracted to an outside organisation.

**Understand the words**

- **Contracts out:** Makes a contractual arrangement with another provider.
- **Specific business function:** A particular activity or function, not necessarily the entire business.
- **External provider:** An organisation outside the business.

*Example:* A Nepalese company contracts an external IT company to manage its IT infrastructure instead of performing that function entirely with its own employees.

This is a teaching example to help you understand the definition.

### 2. Benefits of Outsourcing IT

The study material identifies two main benefits.

#### Benefit 1 - Focus on Core Activities

Outsourcing allows the business to focus on its core activities.

What does this mean? A business has limited management time, attention and resources. By contracting out a specific function, it can focus more on the activities central to its business.

*Example:* A bank focuses on banking services while an external provider handles a contracted IT function.

#### Benefit 2 - Access to Better IT Functions and Services

Outsourcing provides access to better IT functions and services than may be achievable with in-house staff.

What does this mean? An external specialist may provide IT functions and services that the organisation might not be able to achieve using its own employees.

*Example:* A small business contracts a specialist technology provider because it cannot achieve the same level of IT service using its existing in-house team.

**Remember:** Outsourcing benefits = Focus + Better IT services.

### 3. Challenges of Outsourcing

Outsourcing is not automatically beneficial. The study material identifies three important challenges.

#### 1. Increased Formality in Managing the IT Function

When IT is outsourced, the organisation needs a more formal approach to managing the relationship and the service arrangement.

**Simple understanding:** The organisation cannot manage the external provider as casually as it might manage its own internal employees.

#### 2. Service Managed at Arm's Length - Reduced Direct Control

The service is managed at arm's length, which means the organisation has reduced direct control over the way the external provider performs the service.

*Example:* The bank depends on an external IT provider to perform the contracted function. It does not directly manage every activity of that provider.

#### 3. Contractual Issues - Service Level Agreements (SLAs)

Contractual issues, such as Service Level Agreements (SLAs), must be carefully negotiated.

What is the key idea? The organisation and external provider need to carefully negotiate the contractual arrangements governing the service.

*Example:* Before outsourcing its IT service, a bank carefully negotiates the SLA with the provider.

### 4. Understand the important differences

Students sometimes confuse outsourcing with other arrangements.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Outsourcing | Contracting a specific business function to an external provider. |
| Off-shoring | Moving work to another country; it does not necessarily involve an external provider. |
| Privatisation | Transferring public-sector activities to the private sector. |
| Shared Service Centre | An internal centre serving multiple business units; the activity remains in-house. |

These distinctions are also reflected in the study material's answer explanations.

**Exam trap:** Work being performed in another country does not automatically mean outsourcing. The key question is whether the function has been contracted to an external provider.

### 5. Benefits vs Challenges - Quick Comparison

| Benefits | Challenges |
|---|---|
| Focus on core activities | Increased formality |
| Access to better IT services | Reduced direct control |
| | Careful negotiation of SLAs |

A simple memory trick:

- **Benefits:** Why should we outsource?
- **Challenges:** What difficulties arise after outsourcing?

## Topic 1.8 - Innovation

**Section 1 · IT in the Business Environment | Concept + business application**

This topic is short, but it carries an important business message:

**Technology keeps changing. Businesses must innovate and invest in new technology if they want to remain competitive.**

Let's understand why.

### 1. Start with a business situation

Imagine two companies operating in the same industry.

- **Company A** continues using old technology and traditional processes.
- **Company B** invests in new technology and uses IT to improve its business activities.

As technology develops, Company B may gain advantages over Company A.

**What is the lesson?**

A business cannot simply depend on the technology it already has. It must keep evaluating new developments and invest in technology where appropriate.

### 2. Why is innovation important?

The study material explains:

> Due to the rapid pace of change in IT, businesses must invest in new technology to avoid being left behind by competitors who use IT for competitive advantage.

Let's break this into three parts.

**1. Rapid pace of change in IT**

Technology develops continuously. New technologies and capabilities become available.

**2. Businesses must invest in new technology**

Organisations need to consider investing in relevant technological developments rather than relying entirely on existing systems.

**3. Avoid being left behind by competitors**

Competitors may use IT to gain a competitive advantage. Businesses that fail to adapt risk falling behind.

**IT Innovation → Competitive Advantage**

Businesses invest in relevant new technology to remain competitive.

**Simple Nepali-English understanding:** Technology change भइरहन्छ। Business ले नयाँ technology adopt गर्न सकेन भने competitors अगाडि जान सक्छन्।

### 3. Innovation in Practice - Two Important Examples

The study material provides two examples. Understand both carefully because they demonstrate different ways technology can create business value.

## Topic 1.9 - Information Processing: CATIVA

**Section 1 · Final topic | Important for MCQs**

This is the final topic of Section 1: IT in the Business Environment.

Let's begin with a simple question:

**When a business collects raw data, is that data automatically useful to management?**

No. Raw data must be processed into meaningful information that can be communicated and used for decision-making.

However, processing alone is not enough. The processing must satisfy certain criteria to ensure that the resulting information is valuable and effective.

These six criteria are remembered through the mnemonic **CATIVA**.

### 1. First understand Information Processing

**From Data to Information**

- **DATA:** Raw facts and figures collected by the business
- **Information Processing:** Converting collected data into information
- **INFORMATION:** Meaningful output communicated within the business

**Key term - Information Processing**

Data, once collected, is converted into information for communicating more widely within the business.

The effectiveness of this process is assessed using six criteria.

### 2. CATIVA - Six criteria of effective information processing

**C A T I V A**

| Letter | Criterion |
|---|---|
| C | Completeness |
| A | Accuracy |
| T | Timeliness |
| I | Inalterability |
| V | Verifiability |
| A | Assessability |

#### C - Completeness

**Meaning:** Everything that needs to be processed must be processed. Nothing must be omitted.

Imagine a company has 10 branches and prepares a sales report. If the report includes information from only 8 branches and omits the remaining 2 branches, the processing is incomplete.

**Remember:** Completeness = Nothing important omitted.

**Example:** All relevant branch sales data must be processed.

#### A - Accuracy

**Meaning:** Processing ensures that data remains true to its sources, and the information produced contains no errors.

Imagine the original sales records show NPR 5,00,000, but the final report shows NPR 5,50,000 because of a processing error. The resulting information is inaccurate.

**Remember:** Accuracy = Correct information, without errors.

**Example:** Processed figures must correctly reflect the original source data.

#### T - Timeliness

**Meaning:** Processing must occur in line with data availability and information needs. In many cases, this means real time (instantaneously).

Imagine a bank needs information about suspicious transactions. If the information is processed only after several days, it may not meet the bank's immediate information needs.

**Remember:** Timeliness = Process information when needed.

**Example:** Processing transaction information in real time where immediate information is required.

#### I - Inalterability

**Meaning:** The process cannot be altered by unauthorised persons while in action, nor can it be altered once completed.

Imagine an organisation has an approved payroll processing system. An unauthorised employee should not be able to change the processing procedure while it is running or alter the completed process.

This criterion helps both accuracy and security.

**Remember:** Inalterability = Processing cannot be changed without authorisation.

**Example:** Restricting changes to authorised persons.

#### V - Verifiability

**Meaning:** The data trail from the source, through processing, to the resulting information can be followed.

This is also known as the **audit trail**.

Imagine an auditor reviewing a company's reported revenue. The auditor should be able to trace:

Original sales invoices → Accounting records → Processing → Revenue report.

If the trail can be followed, the processing is verifiable.

**Remember:** Verifiability = Traceability = Audit trail.

**Example:** Following data from its original source through to the final report.

#### A - Assessability

**Meaning:** The effectiveness of processing is open to scrutiny. Its quality can therefore be judged.

Imagine management wants to evaluate whether the company's information processing system is effective. The processing should be open to examination so that its quality and effectiveness can be assessed.

**Remember:** Assessability = Open to scrutiny and evaluation.

**Example:** Reviewing the processing system to judge its effectiveness.

### 3. The most important distinctions - Don't confuse these!

This is where students often make mistakes in MCQs.

#### Accuracy vs Inalterability

| Accuracy | Inalterability |
|---|---|
| Information contains no errors. | Processing cannot be changed by unauthorised persons. |
| Focuses on correctness. | Focuses on protection against unauthorised changes. |
| Incorrect figures indicate failure. | Unauthorised changes indicate failure. |

**Example:**

- Report contains incorrect figures → Accuracy failure.
- Unauthorised employee changes the processing procedure → Inalterability failure.

#### Verifiability vs Assessability

| Verifiability | Assessability |
|---|---|
| Data trail can be followed. | Processing effectiveness can be scrutinised. |
| Focuses on traceability. | Focuses on evaluation. |
| Also known as audit trail. | Quality can be judged. |

**Example:**

- Auditor traces a figure from the source document to the final report → Verifiability.
- Auditor evaluates whether the processing system is effective → Assessability.

#### Completeness vs Accuracy

| Completeness | Accuracy |
|---|---|
| Nothing required is omitted. | Information is correct. |
| Focuses on coverage. | Focuses on correctness. |

**Example:**

- Two branches are missing from the report → Completeness.
- All branches are included, but their figures are wrong → Accuracy.

### 4. CATIVA in one business scenario

Imagine a company prepares a monthly sales report for its management.

| Situation | CATIVA criterion |
|---|---|
| All branch data is included. | Completeness |
| Figures correctly reflect source records. | Accuracy |
| Report is processed when management needs it. | Timeliness |
| Unauthorised people cannot change the process. | Inalterability |
| Figures can be traced back to original records. | Verifiability |
| The effectiveness of processing can be examined. | Assessability |

**One-line memory story**

A good information processing system must process everything, process it correctly, process it on time, protect the process from unauthorised changes, allow the data trail to be traced, and permit its effectiveness to be evaluated.

**CATIVA = Complete · Correct · Timely · Protected · Traceable · Assessable**

The final six-word phrase is a teaching-created memory aid, not a replacement for the official criterion names.

### 5. Important exam traps

- **Non-repudiation is NOT a CATIVA criterion.** Non-repudiation is a security quality, but it is not one of the six information-processing criteria.
- **Integrity is also not a separate CATIVA criterion.** In the security context, integrity means that stored data is the same as its source and has not been altered.

Remember these two exam distinctions:

- Errors in information → **Accuracy**.
- Restricting changes to authorised persons → **Inalterability**.

## Topic 2.1 - Who Uses Financial Information?

#### 1. Start with a business situation

Imagine Himalayan Manufacturing Ltd. has prepared its annual financial statements.

The company has:

- NPR 50 crore in revenue.
- NPR 6 crore in profit.
- NPR 25 crore in total assets.
- NPR 12 crore in borrowings.

Who would be interested in these figures? Think about it:

- An investor wants to know whether to purchase shares.
- A bank wants to know whether the company can repay its loan.
- An employee wants to know whether the company is financially stable.
- A supplier wants to know whether the company will pay its outstanding bills.
- The government wants information for taxation and regulation.

Different users need financial information for different reasons.

#### 2. Primary users under the NFRS Framework

The NFRS Framework identifies two groups as the primary users of general-purpose financial reports.

**Two Primary User Groups**

- **Investors:** Present and potential shareholders
- **Lenders and Other Creditors:** Providers of debt and credit

##### A. Investors - Present and Potential Shareholders

Investors use financial information for four main purposes.

**1. Buying, selling or holding equity shares**

Investors evaluate financial information before deciding whether to buy, sell or hold shares.

*Example:* An investor examines the company's profitability before purchasing its shares.

**2. Assessing risk and return**

Investors want to understand the possible returns and risks associated with their investment.

*Example:* An investor compares the company's profitability and financial position before investing.

**3. Assessing the ability to pay dividends**

Shareholders want to understand whether the company has the ability to distribute dividends.

*Example:* An investor examines profitability and financial information before expecting dividend income.

**4. Financial due diligence before a major acquisition**

Before acquiring a major or controlling stake, an investor examines the company's financial information carefully.

*Example:* A potential investor reviews financial statements before purchasing a controlling interest in a company.

##### B. Lenders and Other Creditors

Lenders and creditors are interested in whether the company can meet its financial obligations.

Their key information needs include:

**1. Decisions on buying, selling or holding debt instruments**

*Example:* A lender evaluates whether to purchase or continue holding a company's bonds.

**2. Assessing whether loans and interest will be repaid when due**

*Example:* A bank examines the company's financial position before approving a loan.

**3. Assessing creditworthiness before extending credit**

*Example:* A supplier or lender evaluates whether the company is financially reliable enough to receive credit.

**Remember the distinction:**

- Investors → Equity investment, risk, return and dividends.
- Lenders and other creditors → Debt, repayment, interest and creditworthiness.

#### 3. Other users of financial information

Apart from primary users, several other groups also have information needs.

| User | What do they want to know? |
|---|---|
| Employees | Employer's stability and profitability; ability to provide remuneration, employment and retirement benefits. |
| Customers | Whether the business will continue to exist, particularly where they depend on it over the long term. |
| Suppliers and Business Partners | Likelihood of being paid when due. |
| Governments and Regulators | Resource allocation, regulation, taxation income and national statistics. |
| Public and Community Representatives | Trends in business prosperity, particularly where the business contributes substantially to the local economy. |

**Understand through examples**

- **Employees:** Will the company remain financially stable and continue providing employment?
- **Customers:** Will the manufacturer continue operating and supplying products?
- **Suppliers:** Will the company pay its outstanding credit purchases?
- **Government:** What is the company's taxable income?
- **Community:** Is the company contributing to economic prosperity in the area?

**Exam alert**

Financial statements are not prepared only for internal users. They are useful to both internal and external parties, including investors, lenders, management and other stakeholders.

## Topic 2.2 - When Is Financial Information Useful?

#### 1. The central concept

Financial information is useful when it achieves two objectives:

**1. Decision-making**

Helps users make informed decisions.

*Example:* An investor decides whether to purchase shares after examining the company's financial statements.

**2. Management Stewardship**

Shows the results of management's stewardship of the resources entrusted to them.

*Example:* Shareholders examine financial statements to evaluate how management has used the company's resources.

**What is Stewardship?**

This is a very important accounting concept.

Stewardship means management's responsibility to look after and use the resources entrusted to it appropriately.

Imagine shareholders invest NPR 10 crore in a company. Management is entrusted with those resources. Shareholders want to know:

- How did management use the money?
- Did the company generate profits?
- What assets and liabilities does the company have?
- How well did management perform?

Financial information helps users evaluate management's stewardship.

**Simple understanding:** Management uses resources entrusted by others. Financial information helps users evaluate how responsibly and effectively those resources have been managed.

#### 2. Two underlying assumptions

For financial information to meet its objectives, it must be prepared on the basis of two fundamental underlying assumptions.

| Assumption | Key idea |
|---|---|
| **Accruals Basis** | When it occurs: Transactions are recognised when they occur, not when cash is received or paid. |
| **Going Concern** | Continue operating: The business is assumed to continue in operation for the foreseeable future. |

##### A. Accruals Basis

Under the accruals basis, the effects of transactions are recognised when they occur, not when cash is received or paid.

*Example:* A company sells goods worth NPR 2,00,000 on credit on 25 December. The customer pays on 15 January. Under the accruals basis, the sale is recognised in December when the transaction occurs, not in January when cash is received.

**Remember:** Accruals = Recognise when the transaction occurs, not when cash moves.

##### B. Going Concern

The business is assumed to continue operating for the foreseeable future.

*Example:* A company purchases machinery for NPR 50 lakh. Under the going concern assumption, the company normally expects to use the machinery in its operations over its useful life rather than assume the business will immediately close down.

**Remember:** Going Concern = Business continues operating.

**Exam trap**

The two underlying assumptions are:

1. Accruals Basis.
2. Going Concern.

Do not confuse them with Prudence or Materiality. Those are different concepts.

#### 3. Three key financial statements

Students must understand what each financial statement communicates.

**1. Statement of Financial Position**

Shows financial position: assets, liabilities and equity at a particular point in time. *(Position at a date)*

**2. Statement of Profit or Loss**

Shows financial performance: income and expenses over a period. *(Performance over a period)*

**3. Statement of Cash Flows**

Shows changes in financial position: how cash was generated and used during the period. *(Cash generated and used)*

**A very important distinction**

| Statement | Main question answered |
|---|---|
| Financial Position | What does the company own and owe at a particular date? |
| Profit or Loss | How much income and expense occurred during the period? |
| Cash Flows | Where did cash come from and where did it go? |

**Exam alert:** The Statement of Profit or Loss and Statement of Financial Position help show the results of management's stewardship. Do not describe them simply as forward-looking information.

#### Topic 2.2 - Quick revision

- Financial information supports decision-making.
- It helps users evaluate management's stewardship.
- Underlying assumptions = Accruals + Going Concern.
- Financial Position = Position at a point in time.
- Profit or Loss = Performance over a period.
- Cash Flows = Cash generated and used.

## Topic 2.3 - Qualitative Characteristics of Financial Information (NFRS)

**Very important conceptual topic.** This is one of the most important topics in Section 2.

#### 1. What are qualitative characteristics?

Imagine a company publishes financial statements. But the information is:

- Irrelevant to users' decisions.
- Biased.
- Difficult to understand.
- Impossible to compare with previous years.

Would those financial statements be useful? Not necessarily.

**Qualitative characteristics are the qualities that make financial information useful to users.**

The NFRS Framework divides these characteristics into two groups:

| Group | Purpose | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| **Fundamental** | Must be present | 1. Relevance 2. Faithful Representation |
| **Enhancing** | Increase usefulness | 1. Understandability 2. Comparability 3. Verifiability 4. Timeliness |

The easiest way to remember:

- **Fundamental** = The two essential characteristics.
- **Enhancing** = Four characteristics that improve the usefulness of information.

#### 2. Fundamental qualitative characteristics

##### A. Relevance

**Definition:** Information is relevant when it is capable of making a difference in users' decisions.

For example, an investor is considering purchasing shares in a company. Information about its profitability, financial position and future prospects may influence the investor's decision. That information is relevant.

Relevance has three important concepts:

**1. Predictive Value**

Information can be used as an input to predict future outcomes.

*Example:* Past sales and profitability information helps an investor estimate future business performance.

**2. Confirmatory Value**

Information confirms or changes previous evaluations.

*Example:* An investor previously expected the company to perform well. The latest financial statements confirm or change that expectation.

**3. Materiality**

Information is material if omitting or misstating it could influence users' decisions.

*Example:* A significant undisclosed liability may influence an investor's decision.

**Remember:** Relevance = Makes a difference in decisions. Predictive Value + Confirmatory Value + Materiality.

**Important exam distinction:** Materiality is an aspect of Relevance.

##### B. Faithful Representation

**Definition:** Financial information must faithfully represent the entity's economic phenomena in words and numbers.

For faithful representation, three qualities are important:

**1. Complete**

Includes all necessary descriptions and explanations.

*Example:* A financial statement includes all necessary information about a significant transaction.

**2. Neutral**

Information must not be biased in its selection or presentation.

*Example:* Management must not deliberately present information to make poor performance look better.

**3. Free from Error**

There should be no errors or omissions in the description or process used to produce the information.

*Example:* Financial figures should be calculated and presented without errors.

**What is Prudence?**

Prudence means exercising caution when making judgements under conditions of uncertainty.

The study material explains that neutrality is supported by the exercise of prudence.

**Remember:** Faithful Representation = Complete + Neutral + Free from Error.

**Relevance vs Faithful Representation**

| Relevance | Faithful Representation |
|---|---|
| Makes a difference in decisions. | Represents economic phenomena faithfully. |
| Predictive and confirmatory value. | Complete, neutral and free from error. |
| Materiality is an aspect. | Prudence supports neutrality. |

#### 3. Enhancing qualitative characteristics

These characteristics increase the usefulness of financial information.

##### A. Understandability

Information should be clear and concise. Users are assumed to have reasonable knowledge of business and economic affairs.

*Example:* A financial statement should be presented clearly so that users with reasonable business knowledge can understand it.

**Exam alert:** Relevant information must not be excluded merely because it is complex.

##### B. Comparability

Like transactions must be treated consistently:

- Throughout the business.
- Over time.
- Across different businesses.

*Example:* A company uses the same depreciation policy consistently over five years. This supports comparability because users can compare financial results across those years.

##### C. Verifiability

Different knowledgeable and independent observers could reach consensus that a particular depiction is a faithful representation.

*Example:* Two independent auditors examine the same supporting evidence and can agree that the reported transaction is faithfully represented.

Stating underlying assumptions also improves verifiability.

##### D. Timeliness

Information must be available to decision-makers in time to influence their decisions.

Generally, older information is less useful. However, some information may remain useful long after the reporting period, such as information used for trend analysis.

**Enhancing = U C V T**

- **Understandability** → Clear and concise.
- **Comparability** → Consistent treatment.
- **Verifiability** → Independent observers can reach consensus.
- **Timeliness** → Available in time for decisions.

#### 4. The most important exam comparisons

| Situation | Correct characteristic |
|---|---|
| Information could influence an investor's decision. | Relevance |

## Topic 2.4 - Cost Constraint and Limitations of Financial Reporting

#### 1. The Cost Constraint

Financial information is useful, but preparing it involves costs.

For example, a company may need to spend money on:

- Accounting employees.
- Auditing.
- Information systems.
- Data collection and processing.
- Preparation and distribution of financial reports.

So, should a business prepare every possible piece of financial information regardless of cost? No.

**The benefits derived from information for all users should justify the cost of providing it.**

**Simple example**

A company considers preparing an additional report.

| Particulars | Amount |
|---|---|
| Expected benefit to users | NPR 1,00,000 |
| Cost of preparing report | NPR 30,000 |
| **Net benefit** | **NPR 70,000** |

The benefits exceed the cost, so preparing the information may be worthwhile.

Now consider another report:

| Particulars | Amount |
|---|---|
| Expected benefit to users | NPR 20,000 |
| Cost of preparing report | NPR 50,000 |
| **Net benefit** | **(NPR 30,000)** |

The cost exceeds the expected benefit. The information may not justify the cost of providing it.

**Remember:** Cost Constraint = Benefits should justify the cost of providing information.

#### 2. Five limitations of financial reporting

This is an important section for written answers and scenario-based MCQs. The study material identifies five limitations.

**1. Highly Aggregated**

Many transactions are condensed into a small number of figures. This makes it difficult to evaluate the individual components of the business.

*Example:* A company's financial statements show total administrative expenses of NPR 50 lakh. But management may want to know the individual amounts spent on rent, electricity, salaries and travel. The total figure does not provide all that detailed information.

**Remember:** Many transactions → Few summarised figures → Difficult to examine individual components.

**2. Backward-Looking**

Financial statements cover a period that has already ended. However, most users make decisions based on future expectations rather than historical results.

Financial information can:

- Identify trends.
- Confirm previous expectations.

But it cannot provide all the information users need.

*Example:* A financial statement for FY 2082/83 shows last year's performance. An investor deciding whether to invest in FY 2083/84 also needs to consider future expectations.

**3. Omits Non-Financial Data**

Financial statements do not contain all non-financial information about the business.

They may not include:

- Narrative descriptions of major operations.
- Discussions of business risks and opportunities.
- Information about governance or management policies.

These matters are covered separately in the strategic report.

*Example:* Financial statements may show revenue and profit, but may not fully explain the company's employee turnover, operational risks or management policies.

**4. Historical Cost Basis**

Transactions are recorded at cost without accounting for inflation. As time passes, asset and liability values may become misleading. This is especially problematic when inflation rates are high.

*Example:* A company purchased land for NPR 20 lakh many years ago. Due to inflation and changes in market conditions, its current economic value may be substantially different. The historical cost recorded in the accounts may not reflect that current value.

**5. Standardised Format**

Financial statements follow a highly standardised presentation format. However, businesses are diverse in nature. One standard format may not meet the specific information needs of every reader.

*Example:* A bank, a manufacturing company and a software company have very different business models. A standardised financial statement format may not provide every reader with all the specific information they want.

#### 3. Visual summary of the five limitations

**Why financial statements have limitations**

1. **Highly Aggregated:** Too many transactions summarised into too few figures.
2. **Backward-Looking:** Reports past results, not everything about the future.
3. **Omits Non-Financial Data:** Does not include all operational, risk and governance information.
4. **Historical Cost Basis:** Recorded cost may become misleading over time, particularly with inflation.
5. **Standardised Format:** One format may not satisfy every reader's specific needs.

**What happens when financial information is poor?**

Poor financial information can:

- Undermine the integrity of financial markets.
- Raise the cost of capital for the economy.
- Fail to serve the public interest.

This is why professional accountants have an important responsibility to maintain the quality of financial reporting.

## Topic 3.1 - Spreadsheets

#### 1. What is a spreadsheet?

A spreadsheet is an application that enables users to store and analyse data.

#### 2. Understand the structure of a spreadsheet

A spreadsheet consists of **rows** and **columns**. Where a row and a column intersect, we get a **cell**.

Each cell is identified by a unique column-row reference. For example:

- **A4** = Column A, Row 4.
- **B5** = Column B, Row 5.
- **C10** = Column C, Row 10.

**Exam point:** Cell A4 refers to the cell at the intersection of column A and row 4.

#### 3. Four types of content in a spreadsheet

This is the most important concept in Topic 3.1. Each cell can store one of four types of content.

**Four Types of Spreadsheet Content**

**1. TEXT**

Usually words or descriptions. Text may also contain numbers that do not represent values for calculation.

*Examples:* Account Name, Branch, Employee ID, Sales Description.

**2. VALUES**

Numbers that can be used in calculations.

*Examples:* Sales figures, tax amounts, quantities, expenses.

**3. FORMULAE**

Expressions that calculate the value of a cell. They usually refer to other cells.

*Example:* `=B4+B5` - This adds the values in cells B4 and B5.

**4. FUNCTIONS**

Predefined formulae built into the spreadsheet application. They include arithmetical, financial and statistical functions.

*Example:* `=SUM(B4:B10)` - This adds all the values from B4 to B10.

**Formula vs Function - don't confuse these!**

| Formula | Function |
|---|---|
| Expression created to calculate a value. | Predefined formula built into the application. |
| Example: `=B4+B5` | Example: `=SUM(B4:B10)` |
| Adds two referenced cells. | Adds a range of cells. |

Easy way to remember:

- **Formula** = You write the calculation.
- **Function** = The application provides a ready-made calculation.

A function is a predefined formula, so both perform calculations.

#### 4. Workbook and worksheets

A **workbook** can contain multiple spreadsheets or **worksheets**. For example, a company may maintain one workbook containing separate worksheets for different branches.

*Example:*

- Worksheet 1: Kathmandu Branch.
- Worksheet 2: Pokhara Branch.
- Worksheet 3: Biratnagar Branch.
- Worksheet 4: Consolidated Summary.

All these worksheets can be maintained within one workbook.

#### 5. Flexibility and risk of spreadsheets

Spreadsheets are very flexible, but they must be planned carefully to avoid errors in output.

Why? Because users can create their own formulas, arrange data differently and modify calculations. An incorrect formula or wrongly entered figure can produce incorrect results.

Small businesses often maintain their accounting records using spreadsheets.

## Topic 3.4 - Computer Networks

**Important terminology and classification**

#### 1. What is a computer network?

A network is **two or more computing devices connected together to enable the exchange and transmission of information and resources**.

Imagine an office with:

- 10 computers.
- 1 shared printer.
- A central server.
- An internet connection.

If these devices are connected, employees can share information and resources. That is a computer network.

#### 2. Advantages of IT networks

The study material identifies five advantages.

**1. Improved Productivity**

Networks provide quick access to data and resources.

*Example:* Employees can access shared company files without manually transferring them between computers.

**2. Better Communication**

Networks enable communication through:

- Email.
- Online meetings.
- Messaging.

*Example:* Employees working in different offices can communicate through online meetings.

**3. Remote Working**

Networks support remote working and reduce the costs of office space.

*Example:* Employees can access work resources while working from home.

**4. Enhanced Resource Management**

Data and resources can be centralised and controlled.

*Example:* A company can maintain shared data and resources in a centralised environment.

**5. Increased Flexibility**

Data can be made available on demand.

*Example:* An authorised employee can access required business information when needed.

**Remember the five advantages:** Productivity · Communication · Remote Working · Resource Management · Flexibility

#### 3. Types of computer networks

This is the most important part of Topic 3.4.

The study material identifies four network types: **LAN, WAN, WLAN, VPN**.

Let's understand them one by one.

##### A. LAN - Local Area Network

- **Coverage:** Short distance, such as within a single building.
- **Key feature:** Typically uses physical cables.
- **Example:** Computers connected within one office building.

Think of a company with 20 computers in one office. These computers are connected to share information and resources. That is a LAN.

##### B. WAN - Wide Area Network

- **Coverage:** Wide geographical area, such as across a region or country.
- **Key feature:** Connects multiple LANs over long distances.
- **Example:** A bank connects its Kathmandu, Pokhara and Biratnagar branch networks.

Each office may have its own LAN, while a WAN connects the different locations.

##### C. WLAN - Wireless Local Area Network

- **Coverage:** Similar to LAN, but using wireless connectivity.
- **Key feature:** Uses radio transmission; no physical cables are needed for the wireless connection.
- **Example:** Employees connect laptops and mobile devices to an office Wi-Fi network.

**Important:** WLAN is not the same as WAN.

- WLAN = Wireless local network.
- WAN = Wide geographical network.

##### D. VPN - Virtual Private Network

- **Coverage:** Any distance, over the internet.
- **Key feature:** Encrypted connection that protects user identity and denies access to hackers.
- **Example:** An employee is working from home and needs to connect securely to the company's network over the internet. A VPN can provide an encrypted connection.

**Remember:** VPN = Secure, encrypted connection.

#### 4. Compare all four network types

| Network | Coverage | Key feature |
|---|---|---|
| LAN | Short distance; e.g. one building | Typically physical cables |
| WAN | Region or country | Connects multiple LANs over long distances |
| WLAN | Similar to LAN | Wireless radio transmission |
| VPN | Any distance over internet | Encrypted connection |

**The easiest way to remember**

| Network | Word | Hint |
|---|---|---|
| LAN | Local | Same building |
| WAN | Wide | Across regions |
| WLAN | Wireless | Wi-Fi / Radio |
| VPN | Secure | Encrypted connection |
